\begin{abstract}
A valid signature proves that a key signed a message. It does not say how many
actions an executor may perform with that message. This gap matters when an
untrusted collector can ask honest signers to approve adaptively chosen
requests, retain the resulting artifacts, and present them concurrently to
several executors. Replaying one genuine approval can then cause two provider
entries without forging any signature.

We formalize this gap as \emph{authorization non-amplification} (ANA). In a
finite multi-user experiment, the adversary wins if a provider entry lacks a
prior issuance record or, for any required key unrevealed before entry, lacks
an earlier signing event for the accepted context and slot. The adversary also
wins if one issued instance witnesses more than one provider entry. We show
that neither EUF-CMA security nor signer-to-collector injective agreement
implies ANA.

Our construction combines EUF-CMA signatures, a collision-resistant hash,
injective typed encodings, an ideal exact issue-and-consume resource, and a
one-use provider mediator. Except for setup-key collision, an entry without the
required witness yields either a signature forgery or a hash collision; the
state resources rule out duplicate entry. A separate bound names the registry
and mediation failures that a real implementation must control. Four Tamarin
models illustrate the correspondence, while nine weakened variants produce the
expected attacks. The result concerns provider entry only; it does not establish
human identity, display fidelity, policy correctness, or exactly-once physical
effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Digital signatures answer a message-authenticity question: did a holder of a
particular key sign these bytes? Consequential authorization adds a different
question: how many provider entries may those bytes authorize? The second
answer is not contained in the first.

Consider a service that asks an enrolled approver to sign a transfer, release,
deployment, or control action. The service may be honest, but the security
boundary should survive a compromised collector. Such a collector can request
many genuine signatures, choose the order in which requests are shown, retain
old artifacts, combine signatures obtained at different times, and race the
same artifact through several executors. None of those operations requires a
signature forgery.

This paper studies that collector as the adversary. The adversary chooses the
semantic authorization context, subject to a closed typed grammar, and obtains
genuine signatures from uncompromised pinned keys. It also controls transport,
storage, scheduling, and presentation. The intended guarantee is deliberately
narrow: if an executor crosses a provider boundary, the exact admitted action
must have one prior issued authorization instance and, for each then-unrevealed
required key, a prior signing event on the exact slot message; one issued
instance must not account for two entries.

We call the guarantee \emph{authorization non-amplification} (ANA). For a trace
$T$, let
\[
  \operatorname{amp}_T(d,u)=
  |\{e:e=\Entry(d,u,h,\tau,a,c,S)\text{ occurs in }T\}|.
\]
The cardinality clause is $\operatorname{amp}_T(d,u)\leq 1$ for every issued
instance key $(d,u)$; this is replay prevention at the provider-entry boundary.
The witness clause is separate: it relates each entry to one exact trusted
issuance record and to every exact slot-message signing event required by the
policy recorded in that issuance.

Our research question is:

\begin{quote}
When a collector may obtain genuine signatures on adaptively chosen
authorization contexts, what assumptions ensure that each provider entry is
backed by the exact issued context and that one issuance cannot unlock two
entries?
\end{quote}

We answer it with a finite multi-user experiment in which the adversary wins by
causing an unwitnessed provider entry or by using one issued instance for two
entries. We prove a construction in an ideal-resource model and a refinement bound
that exposes concrete registry and mediation failures. ANA is not a new
correspondence logic. It is a security game for a particular cross-role
correspondence---from issuance and signer events to executor entry---under
chosen-context collection, adaptive reveal, and concurrent admission. The
proof separates three obligations that are often collapsed:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Message authenticity.} Every unrevealed required key signed the
exact accepted typed context.
\item \textbf{Authorization-instance coherence.} Every required signature and
the executor refer to the same independently issued context and pinned policy.
This does not claim that approvers were contemporaneous or mutually aware.
\item \textbf{Non-amplification.} The trusted issue-and-consume record that
witnesses one provider entry cannot witness another.
\end{enumerate}

The distinction is cryptographically useful. EUF-CMA counts previously unsigned
messages; ANA counts admitted actions. Lowe's injective agreement is
parameterized by roles and agreed data and relates protocol runs between
peers~\cite{lowe97}; ANA uses standard correspondence machinery but relates
authorization evidence to an executor's provider-entry events.
Section~\ref{sec:separations} proves that EUF-CMA and a signer-to-collector
agreement claim do not supply the missing entry bound.

The paper makes five concrete contributions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item a finite multi-user, chosen-authorization-context experiment with explicit
issuance, signing, reveal, verification, consumption, and provider-entry events;
\item a witness-based ANA definition that combines exact semantic coverage,
pinned-policy fidelity, and an injective map from entries to issued instances;
\item a construction from standard signatures and hashes plus an ideal exact
issue-and-consume resource and a one-use mediator;
\item a reduction whose multi-user sum ranges over every enrolled challenge key,
not merely the keys selected by a winning policy, together with exact query and
runtime accounting; and
\item separation results and machine-checked case studies showing why a random
nonce, an authentic signature, or signer-to-collector injective agreement does
not by itself make an authorization single-use at the provider boundary.
\end{itemize}

The strongest claims stop at provider entry. A provider may fail, time out, or
perform an effect whose acknowledgement is lost. ANA therefore forbids a blind
retry after an indeterminate entry, but it does not claim exactly-once physical
execution. It also does not prove that a key belongs to a particular person or
that a display faithfully represented the signed action.

\section{Setting and Syntax}
\label{sec:syntax}

\subsection{Stateful authorization-admission schemes}

A stateful authorization-admission scheme exposes the following polynomial-time
interfaces over a totally ordered trace:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.3cm,style=nextline]
\item[$\mathsf{Setup}(1^\lambda)$.]
Create public parameters, certified signer keys, trusted issuer state, and
executor state.
\item[$\Issue(x)$.]
Validate an authorization request $x$, atomically create one immutable issued
instance, emit its issuance event, and return a public instance identifier and
context.
\item[$\mathsf{Approve}(j,u,s)$.]
For certified signer $j$ and policy slot $s$, return evidence over the exact
issued context identified by $u$ and emit the corresponding signing event.
\item[$\Admit(d,u,a,S)$.]
Verify the issued context, action, policy, and finite evidence map $S$; perform
the scheme's one-time state transition; and either refuse or emit one provider
entry.
\item[$\mathsf{Reveal}(j)$.]
Reveal signer $j$'s secret key and emit a reveal event.
\end{description}

Correctness requires that a valid issuance followed by every required approval
and one admission of the exact issued action emits one entry, while a repeated
admission of that issued instance is refused. Security is defined over the
trace in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. This syntax is deliberately stateful:
Proposition~\ref{prop:stateless} shows that a complete stateless realization
cannot satisfy the cardinality clause.

\subsection{Signature and hash assumptions}

Let $\mathsf{Sig}=(\mathsf{Kg},\Sign,\Verify)$ be a signature scheme. For each
enrolled index $j\in[N]$, the challenger samples
$(sk_j,pk_j)\leftarrow\mathsf{Kg}(1^\lambda)$. We use standard existential
unforgeability under adaptive chosen-message attack and write
$\Adv^{\mathrm{euf\mbox{-}cma}}_{\mathsf{Sig}}(\mathcal B)$ for the success
probability of a forger $\mathcal B$~\cite{gmr88}.

For completeness, the EUF-CMA experiment gives $pk$ and oracle access to
$\Sign(sk,\cdot)$ to $\mathcal B$. Let $Q$ be the set of exact byte strings
submitted to that oracle. The forger outputs $(m^*,\sigma^*)$ and wins when
$\Verify(pk,m^*,\sigma^*)=1$ and $m^*\notin Q$. All signing-oracle queries may
be adaptive. This is ordinary, not strong, unforgeability: a new signature on
an already queried message is not a forgery and is not needed by our proof.

Let $H:\{0,1\}^*\rightarrow\{0,1\}^\kappa$ be collision resistant. We write
$\Adv^{\mathrm{cr}}_H(\mathcal C)$ for the probability that $\mathcal C$ outputs
two strings $x\neq x'$ for which $H(x)=H(x')$. Probability is over the
adversary's coins and any public hash-parameter generation. Signature
correctness requires
$\Verify(pk,m,\Sign(sk,m))=1$ for keys generated by $\mathsf{Kg}$ and every
message in the signing domain, except with negligible probability.

The theorem treats the signature and hash assumptions as computational. The
issue-and-consume and mediation interfaces in Section~\ref{sec:resources} are
ideal state resources. Their properties are not consequences of EUF-CMA or
collision resistance; the real-resource corollary accounts for violations
separately.

\subsection{Typed authorization language}

An action $a$ is a value in a closed typed action grammar. Its encoding
$\Enc_A(a)$ is deterministic and injective. A parser rejects unknown members,
missing required members, duplicate members, invalid types, non-canonical
numbers, and invalid text encodings. A transport such as deterministic CBOR or
JCS can implement the wire representation, but the security premise is the
injectivity of the complete typed profile, not the name of the transport.

Formally, let $\mathcal D$ be the tagged disjoint union of byte strings,
canonical text strings, unsigned integers, domain identifiers, audience and
principal identifiers, canonical public-key encodings, slots, actions, policy
vectors, and finite tuples of those types. We require an efficiently computable,
prefix-free encoding $\Enc:\mathcal D\rightarrow\{0,1\}^*$ with an efficient
inverse on its image. Every constructor has a distinct fixed tag, every
variable-length member is length-prefixed, and vectors encode their length and
ordered members. Public-key equality means equality of the canonical key
encoding. $\Enc_A$ and the context encoding below are restrictions of this one
typed encoding. This condition rules out both cross-type ambiguity and
concatenation ambiguity.

A pinned-slot policy is an ordered vector of length $1\leq t\leq N$,
\[
  \Pi=((s_1,z_1,pk_{j_1}),\ldots,(s_t,z_t,pk_{j_t})),
\]
where slot labels $s_k$, enrolled principal identifiers $z_k$, and keys
$pk_{j_k}$ are pairwise distinct and every pair $(z_k,pk_{j_k})$ belongs to the
certified enrollment relation defined below. The theorem is restricted to
all-of-$t$ policies; threshold and other monotone access structures are outside
its scope.

Let $p$ be an immutable policy-version commitment, $i$ the initiating principal,
$r$ the relying-party audience, $d$ the enforcement domain, and $q$ a
$\lambda$-bit instance chosen without replacement by a trusted issuer for the
lifetime of the experiment. Define
\[
  h=H(\Enc(\code{ANA-ACTION-v1},\Enc_A(a))),\qquad
  c=(d,h,p,i,r,\Pi,q).
\]
The context encoding $\Enc_C(c)$ and the outer message encoding below are
deterministic and injective on their closed grammars. No slot nonce is needed:
$q$ gives instance uniqueness and $s_k$ separates slots. The signed message for
slot $s_k$ is
\[
 m(c,s_k)=\Enc(\code{ANA-SIGN-v1},c,s_k).
\]
The admission key is
\[
 u=H(\Enc(\code{ANA-ADMIT-v1},c)).
\]

Choosing $q$ without replacement is an ideal stateful interface, not a claim
that an ordinary random nonce is collision-free. A realization may use a
durable monotone or randomly permuted identifier space, or it must add the
probability of instance collision and adversarial guessing to the deployment
bound.

Enrolled ANA keys are purpose-separated: no signing interface outside the
experiment may produce signatures under those keys on the
$\code{ANA-SIGN-v1}$ domain. Equivalently, every legitimate interface capable
of signing that domain is represented by $\mathsf{SignCtx}$ and emits the same
signing event. Without this premise, a valid signature from an unmodeled
legitimate oracle need not be an EUF-CMA forgery and need not have the witness
required by ANA.
Including $d$ in $c$ prevents a signature gathered for one enforcement domain
from crossing into another. Including $\Pi$ makes the complete set of pinned
slots and keys part of every signature. Including $i$ permits an executor to
enforce separation against the issuer-recorded initiator identifier rather than
against a mutable label.

\subsection{Certified-key model}

The challenger samples all $N$ key pairs independently and defines the certified
enrollment relation $\mathsf{Cert}=\{(z_j,pk_j):j\in[N]\}$ before the game.
If two canonical public-key encodings coincide, it records
$\mathsf{KeyCollision}$ and aborts the experiment. Principal identifiers are
unique by construction. The challenger rejects a
policy containing a pair outside $\mathsf{Cert}$, a duplicate key, duplicate
identifier, or duplicate slot. This prevents a collector from relabeling
$pk_j$ as another enrolled principal. Enrollment, proof of possession, and the
binding from an identifier to a natural person are outside the theorem. The
result speaks about distinct enrolled keys and identifiers, not distinct human
wills.

The challenger also fixes a trusted audience function $\mathsf{Aud}$ before
play. $\mathsf{Aud}(d)$ is the authenticated relying-party identity expected at
enforcement domain $d$. The collector cannot modify this value. An issued
context may carry any audience selected through the chosen-context interface,
but it is admissible at $d$ only when its signed $r$ equals $\mathsf{Aud}(d)$.

The experiment lets the adversary choose the field $i$ in an issuance query and
therefore proves only that no approver identifier equals the
\emph{issuer-recorded} $i$. A deployment claiming initiator--approver separation
must authenticate the initiating principal and require the trusted issuer to set
$i$ from that authenticated identity. It must not copy $i$ from an untrusted
collector.

An adversary may reveal $sk_j$. Authenticity for key $j$ ends at the first reveal
event. A later reveal does not alter a previously ordered trace prefix. This is a
weak-corruption model, not forward security or offline anti-backdating.

\section{Exact Issue, Consume, and Mediation Resources}
\label{sec:resources}

The cryptographic proof uses two explicit stateful resources. Writing them down
prevents a verbal ``single-use'' claim from entering the theorem as an unstated
database assumption.

\subsection{Exact issue-and-consume resource}

For each domain $d$, resource $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{IC}}$ stores a durable map
$R_d$. Each value contains the complete immutable context, not merely a status
bit.

\begin{protocol}
IssueExact(d, u, c, a):
    if no exact prior ValidatedIssueInput(d,u,c,a): return false
    if u has ever appeared in R_d: return false
    R_d[u] <- (context=c, status=unused)
    emit Issued(d,u,c,a) at the same linearization point
    return true

Lookup(d, u):
    return R_d[u] or bottom

ConsumeExact(d, u, c):
    if R_d[u] != (context=c, status=unused): return bottom
    allocate a never-before-used ideal handle tau
    R_d[u].status <- consumed
    emit Consumed(d,u,c,tau)
    return tau
\end{protocol}

Calls are linearizable and durable. The record is not visible before its
$\mathsf{Issued}$ event: storage and publication share one linearization point.
No transition recreates \code{unused}. A successful consume is therefore both
an exact issuance lookup and a one-time state transition. The resource refuses
a caller that presents the right $u$ with different context bytes.

\subsection{One-use provider mediator}

Resource $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{MED}}$ is the only interface that can emit a
provider-entry event. On input $(d,u,a,c,S,\tau)$ it checks for one unmatched prior
$\mathsf{Consumed}(d,u,c,\tau)$ event and one exact prior
$\mathsf{ValidatedEntryInput}(d,u,a,c,S,\tau)$ event, parses action digest $h$
from $c$, and independently checks
$h=H(\Enc(\code{ANA-ACTION-v1},\Enc_A(a)))$. It marks $\tau$ used, emits
$\Entry(d,u,h,\tau,a,c,S)$ once, and refuses every other call. Carrying the
exact action $a$ and validated evidence map $S$ in the trace event makes the
security predicate well defined; $S$ need not be sent to the external provider.
This is complete mediation at the modeled boundary.

The resource models entry, not effect. A successful call means that the request
crossed the boundary. It says nothing about what the provider later did.

\section{Security Experiment}
\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Oracle interface and budgets}

Experiment $\mathsf{Exp}^{\ANA}_{\mathcal A}(1^\lambda)$ fixes $N$ enrolled keys
and gives their public descriptions to $\mathcal A$. The adversary makes at most
$Q_I$ issuance queries, $Q_{S,j}$ signing queries to key $j$, $Q_A$ admission
queries, and $Q_R$ reveal queries. It controls their interleaving and may invoke
admission concurrently.

The challenger provides four oracles.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.3cm,style=nextline]
\item[$\Issue(d,a,p,i,r,\Pi)$.]
Validate the closed action, require every policy pair to be in
$\mathsf{Cert}$, and refuse if an approver identifier equals $i$. Sample $q$
uniformly from the issuer's
as-yet-unused values in $\{0,1\}^\lambda$, construct $c$ and $u$, emit
$\mathsf{ValidatedIssueInput}(d,u,c,a)$, and call
$\mathcal F_{\mathrm{IC}}.\mathsf{IssueExact}(d,u,c,a)$. Refuse if the
instance space is exhausted or issuance fails. The resource stores the record
and emits $\mathsf{Issued}(d,u,c,a)$ at one linearization point; only then
return $(u,c)$.

\item[$\mathsf{SignCtx}(j,d,u,s)$.]
Look up $(d,u)$ and require an unused exact context $c$ whose policy assigns
$(s,z_j,pk_j)$ to slot $s$. Compute $m(c,s)$, emit
$\Signed(j,d,u,c,s,m(c,s))$, and return $\Sign(sk_j,m(c,s))$. Thus the adversary
chooses every semantic field except the issuer's fresh $q$, may obtain signatures
on arbitrarily many chosen issued contexts, and may later discard or splice the
artifacts.

\item[$\mathsf{Reveal}(j)$.]
Emit $\mathsf{Revealed}(j)$ and return $sk_j$. Repeated calls have no additional
effect.

\item[$\Admit(d,u,a,S)$.]
Run the construction in Section~\ref{sec:construction}, where $S$ is a finite
map of presented slot signatures with duplicates rejected,
and the executor obtains its expected audience from
the trusted function $\mathsf{Aud}(d)$. Return its result.
\end{description}

Malformed queries are refused without emitting a security event. The challenger
records a totally ordered trace of oracle calls and resource linearization
points. This ordering is part of the experiment; the paper does not claim an
offline verifier can reconstruct it after key compromise.

The pseudocode below summarizes the experiment. Where it omits detail, the
oracle definitions above control.

\begin{protocol}
Exp_ANA^A(1^lambda):
    generate N certified signing keys independently; initialize empty trace T
    if two canonical public keys coincide: emit KeyCollision; return 1
    initialize F_IC and F_MED; publish public keys and public parameters
    run A with adaptive access to Issue, SignCtx, Reveal, and Admit
    append each oracle event and resource linearization point to T
    if an Entry in T has no exact authorization witness: return 1
    if two Entries in one domain carry the same admission key u: return 1
    return 0
\end{protocol}

The adversary chooses every action, policy commitment, initiator label,
audience, pinned-slot policy, signature query, reveal time, evidence set, and
admission schedule permitted by the interfaces. The challenger chooses keys
and the fresh issuance value $q$. There is no hidden challenge bit. The win
event is an invalid trace correspondence, which makes the game closer to a
computational safety experiment than to indistinguishability.

\subsection{Entry witness}

The following predicate instantiates the generic scheme syntax with the typed
context and trace events defined in this paper. It is reusable by schemes that
expose the same exact issuance, signing, validation, consume, and entry
relations; it is not a black-box claim about authorization APIs that expose no
such events.

Let $e=\Entry(d,u,h,\tau,a,c,S)$ occur at trace position $t_e$. The event has an
\emph{exact authorization witness} if there is a unique prior
$\mathsf{Issued}(d,u,c,a)$ event satisfying all of the following:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item there is an exact prior $\mathsf{ValidatedIssueInput}(d,u,c,a)$ event,
and
\begin{align*}
u&=H(\Enc(\code{ANA-ADMIT-v1},c)),\\
c&=(d,H(\Enc(\code{ANA-ACTION-v1},\Enc_A(a))),p,i,r,\Pi,q),
\end{align*}
whose action digest equals $h$;
\item for every $(s_k,z_k,pk_{j_k})\in\Pi$ whose key was not revealed before
$t_e$, a prior event
$\Signed(j_k,d,u,c,s_k,m(c,s_k))$ exists;
\item $S$ is a finite slot-to-signature map whose domain is exactly the slot
set of $\Pi$,
each $\sigma_k$ verifies under its pinned key on the exact message $m(c,s_k)$,
the signed audience satisfies $r=\mathsf{Aud}(d)$, the policy entries are
pairwise distinct, and no $z_k$ equals
the signed initiator $i$; and
\item one prior $\mathsf{Consumed}(d,u,c,\tau)$ event exists and $\tau$ witnesses
no other provider entry.
\end{enumerate}

The witness does not say that the approvers met, signed simultaneously, knew
about one another, or perceived a faithful display. It proves common
authorization-instance coherence over $c$.

\begin{definition}[Authorization non-amplification]
Adversary $\mathcal A$ wins the ANA experiment if any of the following occurs:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item $\mathsf{KeyCollision}$ occurs during setup;
\item an entry occurs without an exact authorization witness; or
\item two distinct entries in the same enforcement domain contain the same
admission key $u$.
\end{enumerate}

Its advantage is
$\Adv^{\mathrm{ana}}_{\mathcal A}=\Pr[\mathsf{Exp}^{\ANA}_{\mathcal A}=1]$.
\end{definition}

We also write $\Adv^{\mathrm{wit}}_{\mathcal A}$ for the probability of either
the setup collision or an entry without an exact authorization witness.
Separating this quantity keeps key-generation and cryptographic authenticity
risks distinct from the deterministic one-use guarantee supplied by the ideal
state resources.

This definition intentionally gives no authenticity guarantee for a slot whose
secret key was revealed before entry. The second, cardinality clause remains in
force regardless of key reveal.

\section{Separations from Standard Notions}
\label{sec:separations}

We next separate ANA from EUF-CMA and injective agreement. These notions are not
weak; they answer different questions.

\subsection{Minimal worked attack}

Suppose a trusted issuer creates one context $c$ for action ``release invoice
417 for USD 10,000,'' domain $d$, audience $r$, and a two-slot policy. Both
pinned signers approve their exact slot messages. A collector now holds only
genuine signatures returned by the signing interface. If two stateless
executor replicas each verify the same tuple $(d,u,a,S)$ and each submits the
request to the provider, then both signatures remain valid, no new message has
been forged, and the signers' protocol runs remain unchanged. Nevertheless the
trace contains two entries for the same $(d,u)$, so
$\operatorname{amp}_T(d,u)=2$. The attack is an authorization amplification,
not an authentication forgery.

The construction in Section~\ref{sec:construction} refuses the second call
because both replicas share one linearizable consume state. The example
isolates the omitted mechanism: admission must be serialized before provider
entry.

\begin{proposition}[Stateless replay impossibility]
\label{prop:stateless}
Let a complete authorization system expose a stateless admission algorithm
whose acceptance of a valid artifact does not change any state shared with a
later call. If the same artifact can reach the provider boundary twice, then the
system cannot satisfy ANA. For a deterministic complete verifier, the ANA
adversary wins with probability one and produces no signature forgery.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Obtain one valid artifact through the system's issuance and signing interfaces,
then submit it to two admission calls. Statelessness leaves the second call in
the same relevant verification state as the first, so completeness makes both
calls accept and emit entries. Every signature was returned by an authorized
signing interface, yet two entries use the same issued instance. Thus EUF-CMA,
even with perfect message authentication, cannot supply the missing entry
bound. A probabilistic verifier with independent fresh coins and one-call
acceptance probability $p$ yields the same violation with probability at least
$p^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A signed random nonce is insufficient]
Adding a fresh nonce to the signed message does not imply ANA if executors do not
share an atomic consume transition for that nonce.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Replay the complete message, nonce, and genuine signature to two stateless
executors. Freshness distinguishes the message from other messages; it does not
make either executor learn that the other has accepted it.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Byte unforgeability does not imply semantic coverage]
If two distinct reachable typed contexts $x\neq x'$ can be issued and are
accepted under the same signed-byte encoding, an adversary wins the witness
clause using one genuine signing query and no EUF-CMA forgery.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Ask the signer to sign the common byte string while the semantic record is $x$,
then present the signature with $x'$. Verification succeeds on the queried bytes,
but there is no exact prior semantic signing event for $x'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Signer-to-collector injective agreement does not imply ANA]
\label{prop:agreement-not-ana}
Lowe-style injective agreement between an approver and a collector does not
imply ANA at an executor boundary.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take any protocol in which each completed collector run
has a unique matching approver run on data $c$. After the collector completes,
wrap its accepted transcript in an executor API that emits a provider entry on
every call. Replaying that transcript to the API does not create a second
collector completion or a second approver run, so the original injective-
agreement relation remains true. The wrapper nevertheless emits two entries for
one authorization and violates ANA.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[ANA does not imply agreement among approvers]
\label{prop:ana-not-agreement}
ANA does not imply mutual or injective agreement among the approvers.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Approvers may sign sequentially through the chosen-context
oracle without communicating or knowing one another. The issue-and-consume
resource can still give the resulting entry an exact injective witness. ANA
therefore establishes neither mutual awareness nor a peer-to-peer agreement
run.
\end{proof}

The executor event is the missing endpoint in the first construction. ANA can
be written as an injective correspondence from provider entries to issue,
consume, and signing events. The chosen-context experiment turns that cross-role
correspondence into a computational target and identifies the state and
mediation resources needed to realize it.

\section{Construction}
\label{sec:construction}

The following construction realizes the experiment for any all-of-$t$ pinned-slot
policy.

\begin{protocol}
Issue(d, a, p, i, r, Pi):
    validate the closed action grammar and certified pinned-slot policy
    require 1 <= |Pi| <= N
    reject if an approver id in Pi equals initiator id i
    q <- uniform unused value in the issuer's {0,1}^lambda instance set
    h <- H(Enc("ANA-ACTION-v1",Enc_A(a)))
    c <- (d,h,p,i,r,Pi,q)
    u <- H(Enc("ANA-ADMIT-v1",c))
    emit ValidatedIssueInput(d,u,c,a)
    reject unless F_IC.IssueExact(d,u,c,a) = true
    return (u,c)

Approve(sk_j, d, u, c, s_j):
    reject unless F_IC.Lookup(d,u) = (c,unused)
    reject unless Pi in c assigns (s_j,z_j,pk_j)
    m_j <- Enc("ANA-SIGN-v1",c,s_j)
    sigma_j <- Sign(sk_j,m_j)
    emit Signed(j,d,u,c,s_j,m_j)
    return (s_j,sigma_j)

Admit(d, u, a, S):
    (c,status) <- F_IC.Lookup(d,u); reject unless status = unused
    parse c as (d_c,h,p,i,r,Pi,q) under the closed grammar
    reject unless d_c = d
    reject unless r = Aud(d)
    reject unless h = H(Enc("ANA-ACTION-v1",Enc_A(a)))
    reject unless Pi has unique slots, ids, and certified pinned keys
    reject if an approver id in Pi equals initiator i
    reject unless S is a map whose domain is exactly the slot set of Pi
    for each (s_j,z_j,pk_j) in Pi:
        sigma_j <- S[s_j]
        m_j <- Enc("ANA-SIGN-v1",c,s_j)
        reject unless Verify(pk_j,m_j,sigma_j) = true
    tau <- F_IC.ConsumeExact(d,u,c); reject if tau = bottom
    emit ValidatedEntryInput(d,u,a,c,S,tau)
    return F_MED.Enter(d,u,a,c,S,tau)
\end{protocol}

The executor constructs $a$ from the operation it is about to submit. It does
not accept a descriptive action label as a substitute for the provider request.
The policy commitment $p$ identifies an immutable policy version. The audience
$r$ prevents a signature collected for one relying party from being presented
as authority for another. The complete policy $\Pi$ is signed, so a collector
cannot add, remove, or replace a required slot after gathering signatures.
When a deployment uses $i$ for separation of duties, the trusted issuer must
derive it from the authenticated initiating principal as described above.

The construction places validity windows and clocks outside the core theorem.
A deployment may add a typed validity object to $c$ and refuse it under a defined
trusted-time policy. Merely writing a timestamp into $c$ would not prove clock
correctness or anti-backdating, so the core claim does not depend on one.

\section{Security Analysis}
\label{sec:proof}

The proof has three layers. Exact-context binding reduces semantic substitution
to an encoding failure or hash collision. Exact-witness authenticity reduces an
accepted signature without a prior signing event to EUF-CMA. Ideal
non-amplification follows separately from the linear state resources. The final
corollary composes those results; it does not derive state correctness from
cryptography.

\begin{lemma}[Exact-context binding]
Consider two issued triples $(a,c,s)$ and $(a',c',s')$. Conditioned on no
collision in $H$, equality $m(c,s)=m(c',s')$ implies equality of their
enforcement domain, action, policy commitment, initiator, audience,
pinned-slot policy, authorization instance, and slot.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Injectivity of the outer closed encoding gives equality of the literal domain
label, both contexts, and the slot. Equality of the contexts gives equality of
every directly encoded field and of the action digests. If the canonical action
encodings differed, the equal digests would be a collision in $H$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Functional completeness conditioned on correctness]
Assume the ideal resources and condition on every honest signature generated in
this execution verifying under its generated public key. If $\Issue$ returns
$(u,c)$, every slot in the nonempty policy is approved through $\mathsf{SignCtx}$
while the record remains unused, and $\Admit$ is called with the issued action
and those signatures at domain $d$ with the issued $r=\mathsf{Aud}(d)$, then
the first admission emits one provider entry.
Every later admission for the same $(d,u)$ is refused.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Signature correctness makes every pinned verification succeed. The issued
action reproduces the digest in $c$, and the certified policy satisfies the
deterministic structural checks. The first exact consume therefore returns a
fresh mediator token, which the mediator accepts once. The consume changes the
record to \code{consumed}, so a later lookup or consume cannot reach entry.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Ideal non-amplification]
With $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{IC}}$ and $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{MED}}$, two provider
entries in one domain cannot contain the same admission key $u$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every entry consumes one fresh mediator token produced by a successful
$\mathsf{ConsumeExact}(d,u,c)$. Linearizability totally orders consumes for
$u$. The first success changes the unique exact record from \code{unused} to
\code{consumed}; no rule recreates \code{unused}. A second consume for $u$
cannot succeed. The mediator also refuses reuse of the first token.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact-witness authenticity with ideal resources]
Let $\mathsf{Sig}$ be EUF-CMA secure, $H$ collision resistant, and the declared
typed encodings injective. For every probabilistic polynomial-time adversary
$\mathcal A$ using at most $N$ enrolled keys, $Q_I$ issuance queries,
$Q_{S,j}$ signing queries to key $j$, $Q_A$ admission queries, and $Q_R$ reveal
queries, there are collision finder $\mathcal C_H$ and EUF-CMA forgers
$\mathcal B_1,\ldots,\mathcal B_N$ such that
\[
 \Adv^{\mathrm{wit}}_{\mathcal A}
 \leq
 \Pr[\mathsf{KeyCollision}]
 +\Adv^{\mathrm{cr}}_H(\mathcal C_H)
 +\sum_{j=1}^{N}
   \Adv^{\mathrm{euf\mbox{-}cma}}_{\mathsf{Sig}}(\mathcal B_j).
\]
Forger $\mathcal B_j$ makes at most $Q_{S,j}$ signing-oracle queries. Its
simulation generates $N-1$ key pairs, locally answers at most
$\sum_{\ell\neq j}Q_{S,\ell}$ signing queries, performs at most $NQ_A$
signature verifications, and adds polynomial encoding, hashing, policy, and
table work in the stated query bounds. The sum is over the complete enrolled
key universe fixed before play, not a policy selected after a win.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use five games.

\emph{Game $G_0$.} Run the ANA experiment with the ideal state resources and
set the game output to one exactly when setup records $\mathsf{KeyCollision}$
or an entry lacks an exact witness.

\emph{Game $G_1$.} Set the output to zero on $\mathsf{KeyCollision}$. Otherwise
continue unchanged. Hence
\[
 |\Pr[G_0=1]-\Pr[G_1=1]|
 \leq\Pr[\mathsf{KeyCollision}].
\]
The game samples every key independently and handles the duplicate branch
explicitly; it does not condition key generation by resampling.

\emph{Game $G_2$.} Run the identical experiment while recording every input on
which the challenger evaluates $H$; the domain-separation tags are already part
of $G_0$. Abort if two distinct recorded inputs have the same digest. A
challenger that outputs the first such pair is $\mathcal C_H$, so
\[
 |\Pr[G_1=1]-\Pr[G_2=1]|
 \leq\Adv^{\mathrm{cr}}_H(\mathcal C_H).
\]

\emph{Game $G_3$.} For each $j\in[N]$, abort if an entry's evidence set contains
a required valid signature under $pk_j$, no reveal of $j$ precedes the entry,
and the signing
table contains no exact prior row $(j,d,u,c,s,m(c,s))$ for that accepted slot.
Construct $\mathcal B_j$ by embedding its EUF-CMA challenge key as $pk_j$,
generating every other key, and forwarding each $\mathsf{SignCtx}$ query for
$j$ to its signing oracle. It simulates issuance, other signers, the resources,
and all public checks exactly; if any canonical public keys coincide, it
terminates without a forgery just as $G_2$ does. The reduction stops when either
the fixed-$j$ bad event occurs or $j$ is revealed, whichever comes first. In the
former case it outputs the forgery. In the latter, the ANA witness no longer
requires authenticity for $j$, so no subsequent entry can trigger the fixed-$j$
event.

If the $G_3$ bad event occurs first, the accepted signature verifies on message
$m^*=m(c^*,s^*)$. No exact signing row exists. A query on the same bytes under
a different semantic context would contradict exact-context binding in $G_2$.
Thus $m^*$ was not submitted to the EUF-CMA oracle, and the accepted pair is a
forgery. A union bound over the key universe gives
\[
 |\Pr[G_2=1]-\Pr[G_3=1]|
 \leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}
 \Adv^{\mathrm{euf\mbox{-}cma}}_{\mathsf{Sig}}(\mathcal B_j).
\]

\emph{Game $G_4$.} Associate every accepted unrevealed slot with its exact prior
signing-table row. This changes no probability. The exact issue lookup fixes one
context $c$ for $u$; the validated issue-input event binds the exact issuer
input, while outer-encoding injectivity and the $G_2$ condition make every
signed field equal to that context. The mediator's independent digest check
binds the exact action carried by the entry. The deterministic checks enforce
the trusted audience, unique pinned keys and slots, and exclusion of the signed
initiator. Hence every entry has the required witness.

No unwitnessed-entry condition remains in $G_4$, so $\Pr[G_4=1]=0$. Summing
the game differences proves the authenticity bound. The stated query and
runtime costs follow directly from the simulation.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Per-issuance ANA composition]
Under the assumptions of the exact-witness theorem and the ideal
$\mathcal F_{\mathrm{IC}}$ and $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{MED}}$ resources,
\[
 \Adv^{\mathrm{ana}}_{\mathcal A}
 \leq
 \Pr[\mathsf{KeyCollision}]
 +\Adv^{\mathrm{cr}}_H(\mathcal C_H)
 +\sum_{j=1}^{N}
   \Adv^{\mathrm{euf\mbox{-}cma}}_{\mathsf{Sig}}(\mathcal B_j).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The theorem bounds the probability of a setup-key collision or an unwitnessed
entry. Ideal non-amplification deterministically excludes the duplicate-entry
winning condition.
The corollary is therefore a composition of a cryptographic authenticity result
with an explicit state invariant; it is not a claim that signatures create
one-time state.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Concrete interpretation}

If every per-key EUF-CMA advantage is at most $\epsilon_{\mathrm{sig}}$, the
hash-collision advantage is at most $\epsilon_H$, and the probability of a
duplicate canonical public key is at most $\epsilon_{\mathrm{key}}$, the theorem
and composition corollary give
\[
 \Adv^{\mathrm{ana}}_{\mathcal A}
 \leq \epsilon_{\mathrm{key}}+N\epsilon_{\mathrm{sig}}+\epsilon_H.
\]
The $N$ factor is deliberately conservative. It avoids a post-selected two-key
bound when an adversary can range over many enrolled keys and policies.

The theorem is not a durability proof for a database or a code-level proof of
complete mediation. The next corollary states exactly what a real deployment
must add.

\subsection{Real-resource refinement}

Let $R$ and $M$ be probabilistic polynomial-time implementations of the
issue-and-consume and mediation interfaces. Experiment
$\mathsf{Exp}^{\ANA,\mathrm{real}}_{\mathcal A,R,M}(1^\lambda)$ is the ANA
experiment of Section~\ref{sec:experiment} with $R$ and $M$ substituted for the
ideal resources. It records their atomic issuance, consume, validation, and provider-entry
events in the same ordered trace and applies the same ANA winning predicate.
Write $\Adv^{\mathrm{ana,real}}_{\mathcal A,R,M}$ for its winning probability.
In that experiment, define:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $\mathsf{RegistryBreak}$ occurs if any of these conditions holds:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(R\arabic*),leftmargin=2.1em]
  \item a record becomes visible without exactly one atomic
  $\mathsf{Issued}(d,u,c,a)$ event matching an exact prior
  $\mathsf{ValidatedIssueInput}(d,u,c,a)$ event;
  \item one $(d,u)$ has two or more issuance events, including byte-identical
  duplicates;
  \item a stored, returned, or consumed tuple differs from the validated issue
  input;
  \item a successful consume lacks the unique exact issuance;
  \item a successful consume does not atomically emit exactly one matching
  $\mathsf{Consumed}(d,u,c,\tau)$ event, including a different returned token or
  tuple; or
  \item two consumes for the same $u$ both succeed.
  \end{enumerate}
\item $\mathsf{MediationBreak}$ occurs if any of these conditions holds:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(M\arabic*),leftmargin=2.1em]
  \item a provider entry lacks one unmatched prior
  $\mathsf{ValidatedEntryInput}(d,u,a,c,S,\tau)$ event and one exact prior
  $\mathsf{Consumed}(d,u,c,\tau)$ event;
  \item the exact action $a$, its digest $h$, or the exact evidence map $S$
  changes between validation and entry; or
  \item one token witnesses two entries.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

These are trace events in a stated experiment, not undefined labels for
``something went wrong.'' Their probabilities must be justified by a refinement
proof, transactional implementation argument, or measured fault model for the
deployment.

\begin{corollary}[Deployment composition]
Replacing the ideal resources by implementations $R$ and $M$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \Adv^{\mathrm{ana,real}}_{\mathcal A,R,M}
 {}\leq{}& \Pr[\mathsf{KeyCollision}]
 +\Adv^{\mathrm{cr}}_H(\mathcal C_H)\\
 &+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Adv^{\mathrm{euf\mbox{-}cma}}_{\mathsf{Sig}}(\mathcal B_j)\\
 &+\Pr_{\mathsf{Exp}^{\ANA,\mathrm{real}}_{\mathcal A,R,M}}[\mathsf{RegistryBreak}]\\
 &+\Pr_{\mathsf{Exp}^{\ANA,\mathrm{real}}_{\mathcal A,R,M}}[\mathsf{MediationBreak}].
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Conditioned on neither implementation event, the observed issue, consume, and
entry trace satisfies the two ideal-resource safety relations used in the
composition result. Apply that result and then a union bound over the two
implementation events.
\end{proof}

The corollary keeps operational correctness visible. It would be misleading to
call either implementation probability negligible merely because the
signatures are cryptographically strong. This is an in-model resource
decomposition: the environment still reaches the provider only through the
mediator. A deployment claiming complete mediation must separately show that no
alternate provider path can emit an unrecorded entry.

\section{Trace-Prefix Authenticity and Backdating}

\begin{lemma}[Trace-prefix authenticity]
Suppose an entry whose evidence set contains a required valid signature under
$pk_j$ occurs at position $t_e$, no
reveal of $sk_j$ precedes $t_e$, and a reveal occurs later. Except with
probability bounded by the corresponding EUF-CMA and collision advantages, the
exact signing event for the accepted slot precedes $t_e$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Stop the experiment at $t_e$. The later reveal is outside the prefix and does
not change its distribution. The $G_2$--$G_3$ argument from the exact-witness theorem
then gives the exact prior signing event.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Ordinary signatures do not prove offline anti-backdating]
After learning $sk_j$, an adversary can create a valid signature on a message
containing an earlier claimed timestamp. An offline verifier given only
$(m,\sigma,pk_j)$ cannot distinguish it from a signature produced before
compromise.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The adversary runs the honest signing algorithm after compromise. The timestamp
is only signed data; it is not an independently ordered event. Both signatures
satisfy the same verification relation.
\end{proof}

Forward-secure signatures or an independently witnessed append-only checkpoint
can support a stronger statement. Neither is part of the ANA construction.

\section{Symbolic Case Studies}
\label{sec:symbolic}

Four public Tamarin theories~\cite{tamarin} test concrete single-approval, 2-of-2, and composed
instances against a Dolev--Yao adversary that controls the network and can solicit
honest signatures on chosen authorization contexts. The theories are not a
substitute for the computational theorem: they idealize signatures and typed
encoding, and their linear facts assume the state transitions they represent.

The representative positive lemma
\code{injective\_acceptance\_with\_consumption} verifies in 11 proof steps. Its
deliberately weakened companion removes linear consumption and finds the
expected 11-step trace: one genuine artifact is accepted twice. The quorum
model also found an earlier initiator-substitution trace when the initiator was
omitted from the signed context; binding that field removes the trace.

The full result list and nine weakened comparisons appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:tamarin}. The focused models correspond to the construction's
exact-signature and one-use obligations. The composed models test additional
EMILIA reliance checks outside the computational theorem. None of the models is
a mechanized refinement proof of the ideal state resources.

\paragraph{Authentication and injective agreement.}
Bellare and Rogaway's matching-conversation model and Lowe's hierarchy make
session correspondence explicit; Lowe distinguishes aliveness, weak agreement,
non-injective agreement, and injective agreement between protocol participants
~\cite{bellarerogaway93,lowe97}.
ANA borrows the discipline of explicit correspondence events but changes the
relation being counted: provider entries must map injectively to trusted issued
authorization instances. Proposition~\ref{prop:agreement-not-ana} shows a protocol can preserve
signer-to-collector injective agreement while a downstream executor amplifies
one completed run into several entries. Proposition~\ref{prop:ana-not-agreement} shows that ANA permits
asynchronous signers who never establish mutual agreement. We do not claim a
new general logic of correspondence assertions. The new object is the
chosen-context authorization experiment and its exact executor-entry witness.

\paragraph{Computational authorization proofs.}
Wang et al. give a computationally sound mechanized analysis of TPM~2.0
authorization protocols~\cite{tpm20auth}. Barbosa et al. give a modular
computational analysis of WebAuthn and CTAP2~\cite{fido2provable}; Guan et al.
and Schrempp study FIDO2 symbolically, including a human role
~\cite{fido2symbolic,fido2human}. Those works analyze the security of their
authorization or authentication ceremonies. ANA assumes an enrolled signature
interface and places the collector under chosen-context adversarial control. Its
proof target also includes executor entry, and its failure measure is the number
of entries attributable to one issued authorization.

\paragraph{Agent authorization gateways and records.}
OAP intercepts tool calls before execution and produces signed audit records
~\cite{oap26}. aiAuthZ moves identity- and policy-bound authorization off the
agent host and uses an atomic single-use nonce store~\cite{aiauthz26}.
AgentBound describes receipts binding an action to delegation, policy, and
semantic artifacts~\cite{agentbound26}. Llamb\'i-Morillas and
Fern\'andez-Fern\'andez formalize principal, request, context, and policy binding
and identify request-to-runtime execution binding as an open research problem
~\cite{cva26}. These systems already provide pre-action gateways, operation
binding, and signed records. ANA addresses a different question: whether exact
multi-signer evidence remains non-amplifying when a collector may harvest
chosen-context signatures. We answer it with a multi-user EUF-CMA and
collision-resistance reduction tied to the executor-entry event.

\paragraph{Transaction authorization and trusted display.}
Van Herreweghen and Wille analyze which Internet-payment security requirements
an EMV deployment does and does not meet~\cite{herreweghen99}. ``See what you sign'' makes
trusted input and output part of binding a signature to human intent
~\cite{weber98}; Notary builds a trusted device for transaction approval
~\cite{notary20}. ANA does not solve the display problem. It ensures that the
executor cannot accept a different typed action from the one covered by the
signature and cannot admit the same issued instance twice.

\paragraph{Stateful authorization.}
Formal analyses of PKCS\#11 show that mutable state can be integral to a security
claim~\cite{pkcs11state}. StatefulAuth protects authorization policies that
depend on request history~\cite{statefulauth}. Macaroons provide decentralized
attenuation through contextual caveats~\cite{macaroons}. ANA focuses on a
different state relation: exact issue-and-consume state must injectively connect
one signed authorization to one provider entry under concurrent presentation.

The need for spent-artifact state is classical rather than new. Online digital
cash prevents double spending through a bank's spent-coin state, while offline
schemes detect it after deposit~\cite{chaumcash90}. Linearizability supplies the
concurrent-object semantics used by the ideal consume operation
~\cite{linearizability90}, and complete mediation supplies the reference-monitor
principle behind the executor boundary~\cite{saltzerschroeder75}. ANA's
contribution is not the observation that replay needs state. It is the
cryptographic experiment connecting malicious chosen-context collection,
multi-signer typed evidence, exact issuance, and the provider-entry event.

Anonymous counting tokens let an issuer enforce a per-client issuance rate,
including one token per client per message, while retaining the privacy goals of
anonymous tokens~\cite{act23}. That is an issuance-side cryptographic limit.
ANA does not provide anonymity and assumes a trusted issuer has already created
an instance; its question is whether a malicious collector can turn the exact
multi-signer evidence for that instance into more than one provider entry.

SUDP gives an operation-bound, authenticator-backed single-use grant redeemed by
a custodian so the agent never receives a reusable secret~\cite{sudp26}.
Krenn and Lor\"unser use smart-contract state to enforce a user-defined bound on
delegated signature invocations~\cite{singleusesig22}. Both demonstrate that
single use can be built with a stateful redemption boundary. ANA differs in the
adversary and witness: a malicious collector may harvest signatures for many
issued contexts, and each executor entry must point to the exact multi-signer
context and policy that survived that collection.

Lie and Marsh define ledger-authenticator unforgeability for reactive
authorization judged against a finalized transcript, with adaptive corruption
and a multi-user QROM bound~\cite{ledgerauth26}. ANA has no ledger, finality, or
quantum-random-oracle claim. Its trace order comes from the experiment, and its
later-reveal result is only prefix authenticity. In return, ANA makes
multi-signer exact-action artifacts and the non-ledger executor admission event
the object of the reduction.

Xu et al. identify \emph{semantic replay}: a system can consume every token once
and still issue fresh identifiers against the same underlying confirmation until
its action-level budget is exceeded~\cite{caplease26}. Their CapLease construction
uses durable token-independent state to bound reissuance, admission, and recovery.
That result is complementary and sets an important boundary on this paper. ANA
is per issued instance $(d,u)$; it does not stop a trusted issuer from creating a new
$(d,u')$ for semantically equivalent authority. ANA instead gives a computational
chosen-context account of the evidence needed for one issued instance: exact
issuer registration, prior signing events on every exact pinned-slot message for
each then-unrevealed required key, and an injective
map to provider entry. A system that needs a budget spanning several issued
instances must compose ANA with a durable action- or confirmation-level ledger.

\paragraph{Policy-aware and threshold cryptography.}
Policy-Compliant Signatures make policy satisfaction part of an enhanced
signature primitive, and attribute-based signatures hide signers behind
attribute predicates~\cite{pcs21,abs08}. Bellare and Neven formalize
multisignatures in which several certified public keys jointly authenticate one
common document~\cite{bellareneven06}; threshold signatures likewise can make
several shares verify as one signature. ANA instead uses ordinary signatures under
explicitly pinned keys and exposes each required slot. Replacing those signatures
with a policy-aware or threshold primitive would not remove the need for exact
issuance and one-time consumption at the executor boundary.

\paragraph{Non-amplification terminology.}
The phrase \emph{non-amplification} has prior uses with different counted
objects. Maffeis, Mitchell, and Taly require ``No Authority Amplification'' as
part of authority safety for object-capability isolation~\cite{maffeis10}.
Xu et al. use \emph{source-authority non-amplification} to prevent lossy agent
memory consolidation from increasing the trust assigned to source
information~\cite{memoryna26}. Neither property counts provider entries per
issued authorization instance. We use ANA as a descriptive name for the trace
property in Definition~1, not as a claim of priority over those terms.

\paragraph{Concrete formats.}
Comparse lifts bit-string security arguments to typed protocol messages under
verified format conditions~\cite{comparse}. ANA relies on the same lesson. Its
semantic-coverage separation explains why a byte-level signing oracle is
insufficient when an application maps distinct semantic contexts to the same
signed bytes. This paper states typed-encoding injectivity as a premise; it does
not claim a new verified parser framework.

\paragraph{Logs and temporal evidence.}
Transparency overlays and forward-secure audit logs can provide independently
checkable ordering and post-compromise evidence~\cite{transparencyoverlays,secureauditlogs}.
ANA's trace-prefix lemma uses the experiment's event order and makes no offline
anti-backdating claim. A deployment that needs that stronger property must add
one of those mechanisms.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}

The certified-key model does not prove that an enrolled key belongs to a named
person or that two keys represent independent people. It proves distinct pinned
credentials. Directory governance, identity proofing, collusion, coercion, and
one-person-many-credentials attacks remain separate.

The collector may render action $X$ while causing a signing device to sign
the digest of action $Y$. ANA requires the admitted action to reproduce that
signed digest and prevents the artifact from authorizing a different action
$Z$ absent a hash collision. The signature alone does not reveal $Y$, and the
property cannot prove what the approver saw. High-consequence deployments need
a trusted or independently authored display path.

The theorem covers a finite enrolled key universe and all-of-$t$ pinned-slot
policies. It does not cover arbitrary threshold policies, dynamic key-directory
updates, clock synchronization, policy authorship, or transparency-log
consistency. It assumes the executor derives the canonical action from the
operation it is about to submit.

The pair $(d,u)$ is the unit counted by ANA. If an issuer creates a fresh
$(d,u')$ from the same human confirmation or an equivalent action, both instances
may each be admitted once without violating ANA. Preventing that semantic replay
requires durable state and a budget above the per-instance registry
~\cite{caplease26}; it is not implied by this construction.

The negative models analyze synthetic traces and remove one protocol check at a
time; they do not probe or exploit a deployed service.

Most importantly, provider entry is not physical truth. An entry may have no
effect, one effect, or an effect whose acknowledgement is lost. When the outcome
is indeterminate, ANA keeps the authorization consumed and requires separate
authenticated reconciliation. It does not authorize a blind retry.

\section{Conclusion}

Cryptographic authorization needs an explicit unit of account. EUF-CMA counts
new signed messages. A consequential system must also count admitted actions.
Per-issuance ANA makes that second count precise: every provider entry needs an
exact prior issuance and exact signing witnesses for all then-unrevealed
required keys, while one issued instance may not witness two entries.

The analysis separates obligations that should not be collapsed. Signatures,
collision resistance, and typed encoding establish the exact-context witness.
Atomic issue-and-consume state and evidence-preserving complete mediation
establish the one-use bound. The former is a cryptographic reduction; the
latter is an explicit systems invariant. The real-resource corollary keeps
their failure probabilities separate.

This distinction is the paper's result. A collector may complete one genuine
signer run and still replay its output through a downstream executor. Conversely,
an entry may have an exact authorization witness even when its approvers never
communicate. The experiment captures the cross-role relation without claiming
human intent, semantic budgets across fresh issuances, or exactly-once physical
effects.
